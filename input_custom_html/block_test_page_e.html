When to use this one

Variant E is the loud one: a film title card. The photograph behind the type zooms almost imperceptibly for the first half minute, a grain overlay drifts over the frame like projector dust, and the title develops out of blur one letter at a time. Use it where you want a first impression rather than an introduction — a one-off feature page, a yearly retrospective, an announcement that deserves an overture.

How the letter fade works

Two pieces do all the work, and neither needs JavaScript. First, the CSS class .cine-letter: every letter starts invisible, blurred by 18px and nudged down, then animates into focus over 1.3 seconds, with a brief warm glow as it passes through half-focus — the look of a print coming up in the developer tray. Second, the delay: each letter carries its own --letter-delay custom property inline, and the animation reads it with animation-delay: var(--letter-delay, 0s).

The values here were drawn at random between 0 and 1.4 seconds and frozen into the markup, which keeps the page fully static — the scatter is the same on every visit. If you would rather have a different scatter per load, delete the inline delays and let a few lines of script write them: 

document.querySelectorAll('.cine-letter').forEach((letter) => {
  letter.style.setProperty('--letter-delay', (Math.random() * 1.4).toFixed(2) + 's');
});

Everything else is set dressing: the Ken Burns zoom on the backdrop, the tracking on the title settling from wide to final as the letters land, and the tagline, rule and buttons arriving on fixed beats after the last letter. The scroll cue is the last thing in, at 3.2 seconds, after the buttons have landed.

The flash layer that is not here

A title card is exactly the block someone reaches for a projector stutter with, so it is worth saying why this one has none. It used to: background: #fff under mix-blend-mode: overlay, stepped through three stutters with steps(1, end) as the reel starts. Measured against the same frame with the grain switched off, the mean luminance of the stage jumped +3.2, +2.3 and +1.2 levels at 0.60s, 1.25s and 1.38s, each for about 52 milliseconds, across 96% of the pixels on screen.

Three separate things made that read as a fault rather than as light. First, steps(1, end) gave every transition a binary on/off edge — a ramp is read as a change in light, a step is read as an event. Second, a white source under overlay clips: overlay is hard-light with the layers swapped, so for any backdrop above mid-grey the result is 1 − 2(1−b)(1−s), which with s = 1 is exactly 1.0 — pure white, whatever the backdrop was. The bright centre of a photograph is blown out completely, and that is precisely where the title sits. Third, it covered the whole frame, and a global luminance step is far more visible than a local one of the same size.

A smooth warm swell was tried in its place and did not earn the layer it cost. The frame is quiet now — the picture, the scrim, the grain, and the type developing over them — and the picture itself sits back off the stage's near-black through --cine-image-opacity rather than being pushed down by more scrim. The two are not the same move: scrim is a veil laid on the picture, which lifts its blacks towards the veil's colour and flattens it, while opacity mixes the picture into the ground, which keeps the blacks black and pulls the highlights down.

That token is set inline on the section here, at 0.8. The component's defaults are tuned for the front page: a photograph that is mostly bright cloud has to come back a long way before an eyebrow at 86% opacity will sit on it. This mist is already dark, and at the default it went murky — so the page that needs a different exposure sets one value on its own section and changes nothing else: 

<section class="cine-stage" style="--cine-image-opacity:0.8">

With one caveat worth knowing: an inline style cannot be scheme-conditional, so that single value is used in both light and dark, where the component's own defaults differ (0.4 dark, 0.68 light — mixing towards black darkens the midtones fast while mixing towards white mostly flattens them, so the two are not symmetric). It happens to suit this picture, whose warm mid-tone fog reads as a sepia print on paper and as mist in the dark at the same exposure. A photograph that only works at one weight per scheme wants a rule rather than an attribute.

Light and dark

The stage answers to the system colour scheme, which is a departure from the other photographic hero on the site: .hero-stage-photo pins itself light-on-dark because over a photograph legibility is not a matter of preference. What lets this one differ is that the picture is not laid at full strength under a scrim — it is mixed into the stage's own ground. Swap that ground for a pale one and the whole frame goes pale with it, so dark type over it is as legible as light type was over the near-black, and the vignette veils towards the same colour and still holds the edges back in both.

Two channel triples carry it — --cine-ground and --cine-ink — and every alpha in the block is written against them, so the dark scheme is four declarations at the foot of the component rather than a second copy of anything.

The dissolve at the bottom

The frame does not stop at an edge; it gives way to the page over the last 30% of the viewport. The mechanism is the one .hero-stage already uses — a mask-image on the layer that carries the ground — and the reason it is worth copying is that the masked layer becomes transparent rather than fading to a colour, so the page's own background shows through and the dissolve is correct in both schemes with nothing to keep in sync.

That is why the stage itself paints no background: the ground lives on .cine-media, alongside the picture and the scrim, so one mask dissolves all three together. An opaque background on the section would sit under the mask and there would be nothing to fade into. The grain carries its own copy of the mask, offset by the 128px of overscan its box has on every side, or it would go on speckling over the page below long after the picture had gone.

What it costs, and the cheap path

Two of those effects are expensive, and they are the two the title is built from. filter: blur() renders each letter to its own surface and blurs it again every frame, and a dozen of them overlap in time. letter-spacing is worse in kind rather than in degree: tracking is a layout property, so every frame of that settle re-measures and re-wraps fifteen inline-blocks — it is the one animation here that cannot be handed to the compositor at all.

So a small or short screen — the hardware most likely to be the slowest — gets the same choreography with those two properties taken out: the same scattered per-letter delays and the same beats, but the letters fade and rise rather than developing out of blur, and the tracking is at its final value from the start. The stage retunes its padding and type on a short viewport at the same time, so the card fits one screenful on a phone held sideways rather than running past the bottom of it.

The grain is the other thing worth knowing about, and its rate matters more than anything else about it. The noise tile is 240px and the moves are not multiples of it, so every step shows a completely different field — a third of the pixels on screen change value on each one. Film does the same thing, but twenty-four times a second, which is past the point where the eye reads the changes as texture rather than as events. Run it at six steps a second, as this did at first, and the whole canvas appears to flicker. It is eight steps over 0.44s now, about eighteen a second, which fuses.

That it can be run fast at all is down to how it moves: a transform rather than a background-position. The first is a layer the GPU slides, so a higher rate is more layer moves and not one more pixel of repainting; the second would have been a full-screen repaint eighteen times a second for as long as the page is open.

The block honours prefers-reduced-motion and shows the finished frame at once — not by collapsing the animations to nothing but by dropping them, which works because every element's base style is already its finished state and the from-frame lives only inside the keyframes. The card also no longer travels with its own <style>: .cine-stage is a component in main.css now, alongside .hero-stage, because the front page opens with the same title card. Copying the markup above is all it takes.
